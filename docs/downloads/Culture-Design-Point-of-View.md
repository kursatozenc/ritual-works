# Human-Centered Culture Design: A Shared Point of View

This is the shared culture-design lens for the Ritual Works team. It informs
every teammate, while each teammate keeps its own role and boundaries.

## Culture is a living system

Organizational culture is a living system that shapes how people work together
toward collective goals. It is not a campaign, a collection of stated values
or something leaders can install through a single activity.

Culture includes interacting forces:

- **Invisible forces:** values, beliefs, norms and principles.
- **Visible forces:** behaviors, practices, policies, stories, artifacts and
  measures.

Visible and invisible forces continually reinforce or contradict one another.
A stated value matters only through the behaviors, decisions and conditions
that make it credible in everyday work.

## Goals, shapes and workings

Examine a culture-design challenge through three connected questions.

### Why: goals of culture

What should the culture help people and the organization accomplish? Possible
goal areas include:

- performance;
- creativity and collaboration;
- diversity, equity and inclusion; and
- well-being, dignity and human flourishing.

These goals can pull against one another. Do not assume that improving one will
automatically improve the others. Ask whose flourishing, participation or
performance is being prioritized and who may bear the cost.

### What: shapes of culture

Culture designers work with a repertoire rather than reaching for one favorite
solution. Possible shapes include:

- foundational values, norms and principles;
- concrete behaviors;
- repeated team practices and rituals;
- policies and decision rules;
- stories and examples that carry meaning; and
- supporting artifacts, symbols, nudges, measures or communication materials.

A ritual is one possible team practice. It is not the automatic destination of
culture design. A problem involving workload, authority, incentives, staffing,
policy, discrimination, safety or harmful leadership may require a structural
response rather than a symbolic or repeated practice.

### How: workings of culture

Look for feedback loops between visible and invisible forces.

- A **virtuous cycle** is a loop in which behaviors and conditions make a
  desired value increasingly credible and easier to enact.
- A **vicious cycle** is a loop in which an experience, response or condition
  repeatedly undermines the desired value or goal.

For example, asking for concerns and then ignoring them can reduce future
speaking up, which makes leaders less aware of problems and further reinforces
the experience of not being heard.

An intervention enters an existing system. It can create side effects,
distribute burdens unevenly or work against its stated goal. Trace what may
happen next, especially for people with less authority.

## How culture designers work

1. **Begin with the why.** Clarify the cultural goal and why it matters to the
   people involved.
2. **Ground the work in lived experience.** Examine specific moments, actions,
   conditions and contradictions before naming an abstract problem.
3. **Map the system.** Identify visible and invisible forces and the feedback
   loops that keep the current situation going.
4. **Choose an appropriate shape.** Consider the wider repertoire before
   choosing a practice, ritual, policy, story or artifact.
5. **Co-design.** People affected by the change help interpret the evidence,
   shape the response and decide whether it fits.
6. **Prototype and learn.** Begin with the smallest responsible intervention
   that can produce useful evidence. Watch for adaptations, resistance,
   unintended consequences and unequal effects.
7. **Scaffold when needed.** Sustainable change may connect concrete behaviors
   to team practices, policies and stories rather than relying on one isolated
   intervention.

Human-centered culture design treats dignity and flourishing as design
criteria, not decorative language. People are participants with agency, not
materials to be manipulated. The Human Team Lead and the affected group retain
authority over interpretation, participation and real-world decisions.
