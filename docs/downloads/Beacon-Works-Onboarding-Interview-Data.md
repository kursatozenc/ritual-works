# Beacon Works Onboarding Interview Data

## Fictional teaching dataset

Beacon Works, its employees and every quotation in this file are fictional.
This dataset was created for a classroom demonstration of AI-assisted culture
design. It contains no real employee or company information.

The notes are intentionally incomplete and sometimes contradictory. Treat them
as rough interview material, not verified organizational facts.

## Company context

Beacon Works is a growth-stage B2B software company. It grew from 38 to 165
employees in 14 months and now works across three time zones using a hybrid
model.

The early team describes the company as customer-close, low ego, willing to
cross role boundaries and able to move quickly. Leaders want onboarding to
carry forward the useful parts of this “company DNA” as the organization grows.
They are concerned that new employees receive plenty of information but still
struggle to understand how work actually gets done.

## Research question

How does a new employee learn how work really gets done at Beacon Works?

## Interview notes

### I-01 — Product engineer

**Context:** Remote employee, five weeks at the company.

- Received 23 onboarding links. Several pages disagreed about who approves a
  release.
- Their assigned buddy answered questions quickly but cancelled two sessions
  during a busy launch week.
- “I can find the rule. I cannot tell which rule people actually follow.”
- Shipped a small fix during week three. Afterward, they learned that a promise
  made to a customer changed what the expected solution should have been.
- Described the first production review as the moment the company started to
  make sense.
- Does not know whether their first few weeks were typical for other new hires.

### I-02 — Sales manager

**Context:** Hybrid employee, three months at the company.

- Found the founder story in orientation energizing and said it made the
  mission memorable.
- Could not tell which decisions required executive involvement and which
  decisions could be made locally.
- “Move fast sounded like prove you belong before you ask too many questions.”
- Learned more from listening to one customer renewal call than from the sales
  onboarding deck.
- Still asks a long-tenured colleague to explain how a proposed decision is
  likely to be received.
- Thinks some uncertainty may come from being a new manager rather than from
  onboarding alone.

### I-03 — Customer success manager

**Context:** Remote employee, seven weeks at the company; works three hours
ahead of headquarters.

- Informal questions and interpretation often happen in chat after their
  workday ends.
- Their buddy was helpful but worked in another function and could not explain
  their team’s tradeoffs.
- Shadowing two customer calls was the clearest part of onboarding.
- “The how-we-work conversation happens in Slack after the meeting I attended.”
- Created a personal glossary of acronyms and shared it with another new hire.
- Has not raised the time-zone problem with their manager.

### I-04 — Product designer

**Context:** Hybrid employee, four months at the company.

- Enjoyed meeting the founders but felt many early-company stories described a
  world that new hires could not enter.
- “Sometimes DNA feels like knowing company trivia rather than knowing how to
  make a good call.”
- A customer-research archive helped explain why an apparently awkward product
  workflow existed.
- The company values were clear as words. Examples of disagreement and
  compromise were harder to find.
- Wanted to hear what the company had deliberately stopped doing as it grew.
- Said another new designer enjoyed the founder stories and did not share this
  concern.

### I-05 — Engineering manager

**Context:** Joined when the company had approximately 55 employees.

- Said manager preparation varies with sprint pressure. Their last two new
  hires had noticeably different first weeks.
- Uses a home-made “context map” of people, systems and decisions because the
  official checklist lacks team-specific context.
- “We turned the generous early employees into a human search engine.”
- Worries that adding more centralized orientation would make People
  Operations responsible for teaching work that only individual teams
  understand.
- Wants a small, repeatable structure rather than another onboarding meeting.
- Has not measured whether the context map changes a new hire’s experience.

### I-06 — People Operations lead

**Context:** Early employee who helped create the current onboarding program.

- Orientation grew from 90 minutes to nearly a full day as each function added
  more material.
- New hires often describe orientation as friendly but later struggle to
  remember what applies to their first project.
- Buddy quality depends on workload and manager follow-through.
- “We keep adding information because we do not trust the rest of the system to
  carry context.”
- Remote employees ask more follow-up questions about informal norms and
  decision rights.
- Believes the company should retain a shared welcome experience, even if some
  content moves elsewhere.

### I-07 — Product lead

**Context:** Employee number 12.

- Defines the early team’s useful qualities as direct customer contact,
  low-ego debate and explaining the reasoning after a decision.
- Rejects the idea that the goal should be to copy the early team’s exact
  habits.
- “The point was not that we were fast. The point was that the customer was
  close enough to correct us.”
- Believes some informal practices became exclusionary once employees no
  longer knew the same people.
- Still writes short decision notes after contentious product discussions, but
  said the practice is not widespread.
- Thinks some teams need clearer authority, not another cultural activity.

### I-08 — Operations manager

**Context:** Joined when the company had approximately 70 employees.

- Runs a short “first-week show-and-tell” inside an existing team meeting.
- New hires show one thing that surprised them. Team members correct assumptions
  without grading the new employee.
- Finds the practice useful but has not compared the experience with teams that
  do not use it.
- “Veterans should not be the only people allowed to explain the culture.”
- Is concerned that buddy programs repeatedly assign the same generous people.
- One recent new hire declined to present and instead submitted a written
  observation. The manager treated that as full participation.

## Known limitations

- These eight interviews are not a representative sample of the company.
- The notes do not establish how common any experience is beyond the people
  interviewed.
- No direct observations of onboarding sessions or team meetings are included.
- No onboarding survey, performance, retention or demographic data is included.
- The notes do not establish whether leadership, managers or new employees
  agree on what “company DNA” means.
- No practice or intervention has been approved or tested.
