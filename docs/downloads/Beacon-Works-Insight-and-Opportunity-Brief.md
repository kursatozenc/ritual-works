# Beacon Works Insight and Opportunity Brief

## Fictional teaching example

This brief demonstrates how the Ethnographer can move from interview evidence
toward a design opportunity. Beacon Works, its employees and every quotation
are fictional. The brief is a draft for human review, not a verified diagnosis
or an approved design direction.

## Context and intended use

- **Research question:** How does a new employee learn how work really gets
  done at Beacon Works?
- **Evidence artifact:** Eight fictional interview notes in
  [Beacon Works Onboarding Interview Data](Beacon-Works-Onboarding-Interview-Data.md).
- **Intended use:** Classroom demonstration of evidence-to-insight reasoning.
- **Important limit:** No direct observation, representative sample or outcome
  data is available.

## Evidence patterns

### Pattern 1 — Information does not consistently reveal decision logic

Six of eight accounts describe a gap between receiving information and
understanding how decisions are actually made.

- `I-01`: onboarding pages disagreed about release approval; a customer promise
  affecting the work became visible only after a first production review.
- `I-02`: decision rights were unclear; a long-tenured colleague still
  interprets how proposed decisions may be received.
- `I-04`: values and founder stories were available, while examples of
  disagreement, compromise and abandoned practices were harder to find.
- `I-05`: a manager created a context map because the official checklist lacks
  team-specific context.
- `I-06`: orientation has accumulated information, yet new hires later struggle
  to remember what applies to their first project.
- `I-07`: an early employee distinguishes speed from the customer proximity and
  post-decision reasoning that made speed useful.

### Pattern 2 — Access to interpretation depends on people and timing

Six of eight accounts describe context traveling through buddies, veterans or
informal conversations whose availability varies.

- `I-01`: a helpful buddy cancelled twice during launch pressure.
- `I-02`: a long-tenured colleague remains a source of interpretation.
- `I-03`: a cross-functional buddy could not explain team tradeoffs, and
  informal interpretation often happened after the participant’s workday.
- `I-05`: generous early employees have become a “human search engine.”
- `I-06`: buddy quality varies with workload and manager follow-through.
- `I-08`: the same generous people are repeatedly assigned as buddies.

### Pattern 3 — Situated contact makes the culture more legible

Five accounts point to live work, customer context or concrete decision traces
as useful sources of understanding.

- `I-01`: a first production review made the company begin to make sense.
- `I-02`: one customer renewal call taught more than the sales deck.
- `I-03`: shadowing customer calls was the clearest onboarding experience.
- `I-04`: a customer-research archive explained an otherwise awkward workflow.
- `I-07`: customer proximity and short decision notes are described as useful
  parts of the early culture.

## Contradictions, exceptions and bright spots

- `I-02` found the founder story energizing; `I-04` felt that some early-company
  stories described a world new hires could not enter.
- `I-02` notes that some uncertainty may be specific to becoming a new manager,
  rather than onboarding alone.
- `I-05` has not measured whether the context map helps.
- `I-08` reports a useful first-week show-and-tell, but there is no comparison
  with teams that do not use it.
- Existing adaptations include a personal glossary (`I-03`), context map
  (`I-05`), decision notes (`I-07`) and a first-week show-and-tell (`I-08`).

## Interpretation — inferred

Beacon Works may not have an information shortage. It may have a **situated
reasoning gap**: new employees receive rules, values and origin stories, but
the reasoning behind current tradeoffs becomes legible mainly through live work
or access to experienced insiders.

This interpretation would be weakened if direct observation showed that
decision reasoning is routinely documented and accessible, or if a broader set
of new employees reported that the existing materials prepare them well for
early decisions.

## Draft insight

As Beacon Works grows, learning “how we work” appears to depend less on knowing
the company’s stated values and more on encountering the reasoning behind real
tradeoffs. When that reasoning travels mainly through available veterans,
cultural fluency becomes a matter of proximity and timing. This can leave new
employees hesitant while repeatedly burdening the same experienced people.

## Draft cultural goal

Enable confident, low-ego participation in customer-close decisions without
making tenure, location or personal access the price of understanding context.

## Draft human-centered problem statement

New employees making their first consequential decisions need timely access to
the reasoning and customer context behind current tradeoffs, because documents,
values and founder stories do not consistently explain which principles apply
in the moment. They need that access without turning long-tenured colleagues
into permanent cultural gatekeepers or erasing the shared welcome experience.

## Solution-neutral “How Might We” questions

1. How might we make the reasoning behind consequential decisions visible and
   discussable when new employees need it, without turning veterans into
   gatekeepers?
2. How might we help new employees test and revise their understanding of how
   work gets done without making questions feel like a test of belonging?
3. How might we keep customer context close to early decisions across roles and
   time zones while allowing the culture’s practices to evolve?

These questions intentionally do not ask for a ritual. A repeated practice is
only one possible response; clearer authority, documentation, manager support
or another structural change may be more appropriate.

## Unknowns and evidence limits

- How new employees actually behave during early decisions has not been
  observed.
- The evidence does not establish how common these experiences are.
- Differences by function, level, location or manager are not yet understood.
- Decision rights may be a structural issue that onboarding alone cannot solve.
- The costs and benefits of current buddy arrangements are unknown.
- None of the existing adaptations has been evaluated.
- Leadership, managers and new employees may define “company DNA” differently.
